The mechanism everything else modifies

Scaled dot-product attention

Every token becomes three projections of itself — a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I contain?) and a value (what I'll hand over if you pick me). Attention is five steps on those three things.

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax( Q Kᵀ / √dk + M ) V
Step 1 · scoreQ × Kᵀ
Compare the current query with every key by dot product. One query–key pair → one number. All-to-all → a full score matrix.
Step 2 · scale÷ √dk
Raw dot products grow with head width. Dividing by √dk keeps them in a range softmax can use.
Step 3 · mask+ M
For a decoder, set every future position to −∞ so softmax gives it exactly zero weight. Lets us train the whole sequence in parallel while staying left-to-right.
Step 4 · softmaxrow-wise
Turn each query's row of scores into positive weights that sum to one. This is where the tokens compete.
Step 5 · sumweights × V
Multiply every value by its weight and add. Out comes one new vector for the current token.

Two facts that drive the rest of the story

Bill 1 · compute

Every token is compared with every other token. The score matrix grows as T².

6 × 6 = 36

Double the context → ~4× the comparisons.

Bill 2 · KV memory

To generate one token at a time, the model keeps every earlier key & value — the KV cache. It grows as T, and it is private per conversation.

6 K/V pairs saved
Two facts to carry forward. (1) Attention is exact all-to-all comparison — that is both its power and its T² cost. (2) Attention has no idea of order: swap two identical tokens and nothing changes, so position has to be injected separately. Almost every mechanism below attacks one of these — compute, memory, or position — and pays for it somewhere else.

What the timeline is really showing

Long context is a system, not a number

No mechanism here is simply "better." Each bought something and gave something up, and the next paper attacked the new cost. Put an actual target on your workload before you choose — 2K chatbot, 256K agent, 1M codebase — because the right answer changes with it. Two broad strategies fall out of the whole story:

Road 1 · train short, then stretch

Extend the position scheme after training

Train at a length you can afford, then recalibrate positions (NTK-aware, YaRN, and the like) to serve much longer. Cheap — but every extension method has a ceiling; 32× working is no promise 320× will.

Road 2 · build long, then train long

Make long sequences affordable natively

Change the architecture — linear state, sparsity, compression — so training directly on long sequences is practical. No positional-extension ceiling, but you pay in training cost and complexity.

The through-line. A stored absolute position table hits a hard length wall. GQA slows KV growth but never stops it. So every serious long-context system reaches for at least one of: better position, linear state, sparsity, or compression — and pays the matching bill. The skill is choosing which bill you can afford for your workload.
